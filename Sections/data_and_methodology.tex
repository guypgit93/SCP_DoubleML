\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The analysis uses the harmonised Households Below Average Income (HBAI) extract of the Family Resources Survey, a repeated cross-section -- a different sample of households is drawn each year, not the same households tracked over time -- covering 2016--2024, in 2023/24 prices, and excluding 2020/2021 due to COVID-19 disruption. The sample is restricted to children aged 16 or under living in England (the control group) or Scotland (the treatment group); Wales and Northern Ireland are excluded from the main design and used instead as falsification checks (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Table~\ref{tab:summary_sample_composition} reports the resulting sample composition by survey year and country: England contributes the large majority of observations for each year, while Scotland's smaller population share is reflected in correspondingly smaller cell counts, ranging from 537 (FYE2024) to 1,190 (FYE2017) children.

\input{Tables/summary_sample_composition.tex}

Following \citet{andersen2025} and \citet{nesom2025}, the headline design treats the November 2022 expansion as the single treatment date, with the post-treatment indicator refined to the exact 14 November cutoff within FYE2023 where household interview dates are available from the underlying FRS file; the earlier, age-limited 2021 rollout is not used to define treatment timing in the headline specification, since only a small under-6 subgroup is affected and statistical power to detect a separate age-cohort effect is limited (Section~\ref{sec:results} nonetheless reports an age-cohort robustness check where feasible).

The primary outcome is the official DWP child material deprivation (MDCH) flag, a binary indicator equal to one if a child's household is classified as materially deprived under DWP's scoring methodology. For the pre-FYE2024 design used across most of the sample period, that methodology works as follows: a household is asked whether it can afford each of 21 items (12 child-specific, nine whole-family); each item the household reports being unable to afford scores one, and this score is multiplied by the item's prevalence weight -- the proportion of the wider population who already have that item, so that lacking a near-universal item counts for more than lacking a rare one. These weighted scores are summed and rescaled to run from 0 to 100, and a household is classified as materially deprived, setting the MDCH flag to one, once this weighted score reaches 25 or more. 

The questionnaire was redesigned at FYE2024, replacing this 21-item, prevalence-weighted design with a revised 22-item (11 child, 11 household) simple-count design, in which every item carries equal weight and the threshold becomes four or more items lacked. Because this redesign breaks direct comparability with earlier years, this paper instead falls back on ten of the pre-FYE2024 design's twelve child-specific items (the two dropped for sparse or uncertain coverage), each recoded to 0/1 (deprived/not deprived); households surveyed under the redesigned FYE2024 questionnaire, which does not populate these ten items in the same form, are excluded from the resulting item-level measures rather than recoded onto a common scale. From these ten items, two composite measures are constructed on a single, unrevised definition throughout the panel: \emph{any deprivation} (at least one item lacking) and \emph{severe deprivation} (more than five items lacking, this paper's own approximate measure rather than an official DWP statistic). No fixed-count threshold is well established in the literature for defining severity specifically -- \citet{nicoriciuelliot2025} argue against arbitrary count thresholds altogether, favouring latent-class typologies instead -- so this cut is instead motivated by scaling Eurostat's official severe material deprivation indicator, which currently requires an enforced lack of seven of thirteen items (around 54 per cent), onto this paper's ten-item scale (around 5.4 items), rounded to the closest whole-item cut \citep{eurostat2023}. A secondary food-insecurity outcome, available only from FYE2020, is used for comparability with \citet{andersen2025} and as an additional pre-trend diagnostic. The deprivation module is administered on an identical basis across Great Britain, with no country-specific question wording or routing in the FRS instrument, so the measure is treated as consistently defined across the Scotland--England comparison.

Table~\ref{tab:summary_outcomes} summarises the four headline outcomes by country and period. The official MDCH flag and severe deprivation both rise in each country but markedly more in England (MDCH flag: 18.9\% to 26.5\%; severe deprivation: 6.6\% to 8.8\%) than in Scotland (MDCH flag: 14.8\% to 15.9\%; severe deprivation: 4.0\% to 5.6\%). Any deprivation -- lacking at least one of the ten items -- shows a sharper divergence still: it rises in England (36.4\% to 39.2\%) but falls in Scotland (33.4\% to 27.0\%), the clearest raw evidence motivating the difference-in-differences design below. Food insecurity, by contrast, rises by a similar margin in England but stays essentially flat in Scotland

Table~\ref{tab:summary_background} reports weighted background characteristics for England and Scotland pooled across all years, replicating Table 2 of \citet{andersen2025}. Scotland has a smaller share of large (3+ child) families, a similar share of lone-parent households, a slightly younger and more female-headed household-head profile, and a substantially larger White household share (91.8\% vs.\ 76.6\%) than England. 

\input{Tables/summary_background.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:summary_a1_by_period} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-a1-period}) decomposes these same characteristics by pre-/post-SCP period; composition is broadly stable within each country, consistent with the identifying assumption that observable composition is not shifting sharply around the treatment date in a way that would itself explain a change in measured deprivation.

Two covariate sets are used throughout. A lean, six-variable set replicates the CASE evaluation's specification exactly \citep{andersen2025}: a dummy for household head aged under 25, female household head, a five-category ethnicity factor, disability status of the household, lone-parent household, and large family (three or more dependent children). Household income, benefit-receipt flags, and housing tenure are deliberately excluded from this set: none is part of CASE's published specification, and including them carries a risk, since income responds mechanically to receiving SCP and benefit receipt is itself one of the SCP eligibility criteria. A wide, approximately 35-variable set -- continuous income, housing and benefit-unit amounts, family composition, and all nine benefit-receipt flags -- is used only in the double machine learning (DML) analysis (Section~\ref{sec:results}), to test sensitivity to covariate breadth once the estimator no longer requires a hand-specified linear functional form.
 
All estimates are weighted by the dependent-child survey weight (\texttt{GS\_INDCH}); standard errors are clustered at household level throughout, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:lit-identification}. The analytic sample is approximately 47,000 child-year observations for the item-based outcomes and 51,000 for the official MDCH flag.
 
\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
 
The empirical analysis proceeds in five completed stages: (1) baseline covariate-adjusted OLS difference-in-differences (DiD) on the single official outcome replicating \citeauthor{andersen2025}'s results; (2) the same design re-estimated jointly across all ten deprivation items in one household-clustered model; (3) a doubly robust machine-learning re-estimation of the baseline, testing sensitivity to functional form and covariate breadth; (4) the same machine-learning approach applied separately to each of the ten items; and (5) a jointly estimated, vector-outcome version of stage (4).
 
\subsection{Identification: assumptions and treatment allocation}
\label{sec:identification-assumptions}
 
The DiD estimates in this paper are interpreted as the causal intention-to-treat (ITT) effect of SCP on all children in Scotland, not a strict average treatment effect on the treated (ATT), since FRS/HBAI data cannot reliably identify SCP receipt and restricting to self-reported recipients would both shrink Scotland's already-small child sample (Table~\ref{tab:summary_sample_composition}) to an unworkably small size and introduce selection bias, as receipt correlates with the same low income that predicts higher deprivation. The resulting ITT estimate dilutes the true effect on genuine recipients roughly in proportion to the share of Scottish children outside SCP-eligible households, so the DiD coefficients reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} should be read as a lower bound on SCP's effect on the children it actually reaches, not the average effect on a randomly chosen Scottish child.

Interpreting this ITT effect causally requires, beyond parallel trends (Section~\ref{sec:parallel-trends}): correct treatment allocation, no anticipation, and no spillovers between Scotland and England. Scotland is the only UK nation where SCP is paid, making it the only possible treated unit, with England as the natural control under a shared, reserved welfare architecture across Great Britain; Wales and Northern Ireland, which never received SCP, are used instead as falsification checks (Section~\ref{sec:res-placebo}). Scotland also runs other Scotland-only, child-targeted transfers, notably the Best Start Grant and Best Start Foods (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}), so the Scotland$\times$Post coefficient more precisely captures Scotland's wider child-income-support reform package, of which SCP is the largest and only sharply-dated component -- a limitation on causal interpretation partially addressed by the age-restricted robustness check in Section~\ref{sec:res-robustness}.

No anticipation requires households did not adjust behaviour before SCP's rollout in expectation of receiving it, partially tested by the FYE2022 partial-rollout-year exclusion check (Section~\ref{sec:res-robustness}). No spillovers requires Scotland's receipt of SCP does not change England's outcomes; the main threats are cross-border migration -- unlikely at these sample sizes, since relocation costs are large relative to a \pounds25--28/week transfer, and Table~\ref{tab:summary_a1_by_period} shows no unusual shift in Scotland's household composition around the treatment date -- and policy or media spillover, where sustained UK-wide coverage of SCP could plausibly shift England's own counterfactual trajectory (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}). The latter cannot be tested directly, and is carried forward as an explicit limitation in the discussion that follows.

\subsection{Identification: parallel trends}
\label{sec:parallel-trends}

Parallel pre-treatment trends are tested via Scotland$\times$time interactions, replicating the CASE evaluation's own diagnostic tables: a covariate balance table and an outcome event-study table, jointly tested via a Benjamini--Hochberg-corrected Wald test. Standard errors are clustered at household level rather than Government Office Region, since with only around ten regions and Scotland the sole treated one, region-clustered inference is anti-conservative \citep{cameronmiller2015,mackinnonwebb2018} -- confirmed by a placebo test reassigning "treated" status across English regions -- and is independently the correct level given the within-child correlation described in Section~\ref{sec:item-stacked}.

At annual granularity, and under Benjamini--Hochberg correction, every outcome and covariate in both diagnostic tables reads as consistent with parallel trends. Figure~\ref{fig:pt_composite} plots the underlying event-study coefficients: pre-treatment estimates are statistically indistinguishable from zero, visually confirming the Wald test before a treatment effect opens up after the November 2022 rollout. The same pattern holds for the two composite outcomes and food insecurity, reported in full in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-quarterly} (Figure~\ref{fig:pt_composite_grid}).

A separate, finer-grained check replicates the CASE paper's own event-study specification at quarterly granularity, using rolling 13-week windows anchored on the SCP rollout date rather than annual survey-year dummies. Table~\ref{tab:parallel_trends_quarterly_wald} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-quarterly}) reports the resulting joint Wald tests, Benjamini--Hochberg corrected across all 15 outcomes and items tested. At this finer granularity, three series remain flagged even after correction: any deprivation (mdch\_any), Bed/bedroom, and Outdoor play area. This does not overturn the annual-level conclusion for the headline official MDCH flag or the deprivation-count and severe-deprivation composites, all of which remain clean at quarterly granularity, but it qualifies the identifying assumption for the any-deprivation composite and two individual items specifically, an explicit limitation rather than a resolved issue; the corresponding quarterly figure is reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-quarterly} (Figure~\ref{fig:pt_composite_quarterly}).
 
\subsection{Baseline difference-in-differences}
\label{sec:baseline-did}
 
The estimating equation for the single-outcome baseline is
\begin{equation}
Y_{it} = \alpha + \beta\,\text{Treated}_{i} + \delta\,(\text{Treated}_{i} \times \text{Post}_{t}) + X_{it}'\theta + \lambda_{t} + \varepsilon_{it},
\label{eq:baseline-did}
\end{equation}
fitted by weighted least squares with household-clustered standard errors, where $X_{it}$ denotes the covariate vector, which varies by specification as detailed below, $\lambda_{t}$ are survey-year fixed effects, and $\delta$, the coefficient on the Treated$\times$Post interaction, is the DiD estimate of SCP's intention-to-treat (ITT) effect, not a strict average treatment effect on the treated (ATT), for the reasons set out in Section~\ref{sec:identification-assumptions}. Three variants are reported: \emph{Simple} (no covariates beyond the DiD terms and year fixed effects), \emph{Adjusted} (the six CASE controls described in Section~\ref{sec:data}, the genuine \citeauthor{andersen2025} replication specification), and \emph{Extended} (Adjusted plus the child's own age, a labelled robustness variant, since several deprivation items are age-differentiated in what they capture). An item-by-item version of Equation~\ref{eq:baseline-did}, with \citet{benjaminihochberg1995} false-discovery-rate correction applied across the ten resulting tests, is also estimated, alongside an exact replication of \citet{andersen2025}'s own Table 3 specification (which includes an explicit \texttt{Post} main-effect term rather than absorbing it into year fixed effects, and reports the full covariate vector).

Equation~\ref{eq:baseline-did} omits a standalone \texttt{Post} main effect, relying on the year fixed effects $\lambda_{t}$ to absorb the common time trend instead. For most of the panel this is exact, since \texttt{Post} is a deterministic function of survey year; the one exception is FYE2023, within which the 14 November 2022 rollout cutoff falls, so households interviewed before and after that date share a year fixed effect despite differing in treatment timing. The CASE exact-replication specification (Table~\ref{tab:stage1_case_exact}) is identical to Adjusted in every other respect but adds \texttt{Post} explicitly, and confirms this omission does not materially affect the result: $-0.076$ without the standalone term against $-0.079$ with it, both significant at the 1\% level.

\subsection{Item-stacked difference-in-differences}
\label{sec:item-stacked}
 
Fitting the ten deprivation items as ten independent regressions treats each item's error term as unrelated to the others, but the same child answers all ten deprivation questions, so their responses are correlated within household -- a \citet{moulton1990} problem. This stage reshapes the ten items into long format (one row per child-year-item) and fits a single joint model with item and year fixed effects, clustering standard errors at household level so that they correctly reflect this within-child correlation. Both a pooled specification (one average effect across all ten items) and an item-interacted specification (one coefficient per item, with a joint Wald test of the null that all ten item-specific effects are zero) are estimated, in both Simple and Adjusted forms using the same covariates as Section~\ref{sec:baseline-did}.
 
\subsection{Double/debiased machine learning difference-in-differences}
\label{sec:dml}
 
Stage 3 asks whether the Section~\ref{sec:baseline-did} result depends on OLS's linear functional form and narrow six-variable control set, rather than on the treatment itself. A doubly robust, machine-learning-augmented DiD estimator relaxes both without reintroducing the bias a naive substitution would cause, for the reasons set out in Section~\ref{sec:lit-dml}.

The estimator used is \citet{zimmert2020}'s efficient-score, doubly robust DiD estimator, implemented via \texttt{causalweight::didDML()}, chosen over \citet{chang2020}'s alternative for the reasons given in Section~\ref{sec:lit-dml}. For each of the four treatment$\times$time cells, the estimator fits a cross-fitted outcome-regression model and a cell-propensity model, combined into an augmented score that is consistent if either the outcome-regression or the propensity model is correctly specified in each cell, not necessarily both, and Neyman-orthogonal, so that cross-fitted ML estimates in place of a correctly specified parametric model do not bias the ITT estimate at first order.
 
Two nuisance-model methods are compared: lasso, a linear method that (like OLS) cannot represent nonlinearities or interactions, and random forest, which can. Two covariate sets are also compared: the lean six CASE controls (isolating the effect of switching estimators alone) and the wide $\sim$35-variable set (testing whether covariate breadth matters once the estimator no longer requires a hand-specified linear form); feasible here only because of cross-fitting, since an earlier group-time ATT (\citeauthor{callawaysantanna2021}-style) attempt at the same wide set produced singular, rank-deficient models. Resulting ATET estimates for the official MDCH flag are reported under all combinations in Section~\ref{sec:res-dml}, plus a lasso/random-forest ensemble for the lean specification only, since the ensemble did not complete within a feasible runtime for the wide specification.

\subsection{Item-level and stacked machine-learning DiD}
\label{sec:item-dml}
 
The Section~\ref{sec:dml} estimator is repeated separately for each of the ten deprivation items, using the wide covariate set under both lasso and random forest nuisance models, with Benjamini--Hochberg correction applied across items. A fifth stage extends this to a single, jointly estimated model across all ten items using a shared complete-case sample and a multivariate outcome-regression learner, since no existing DML-DiD implementation accepts a vector-valued outcome directly; a hand-reconstructed single-item check confirmed the joint reimplementation is faithful. Both the per-item and the jointly estimated results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
